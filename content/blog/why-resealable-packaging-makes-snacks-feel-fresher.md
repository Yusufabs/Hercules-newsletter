Half of Indian snack shoppers have thrown away a packet that wasn't actually spoiled. Not because the product had gone bad, but because the pack, once opened, stopped looking and feeling trustworthy. That's not a shelf life problem. It's a packaging design problem, and new research suggests it's costing brands more than they realize, both in wasted product and in missed pricing power.

Hercules ran a study of 1,240 verified respondents across Indian metros and tier two cities in September 2026, testing how packaging format alone, independent of what's inside, shapes whether a snack feels fresh. The results point to a clear, actionable conclusion for any brand still shipping foil pouches: the pack is doing more marketing work than most teams give it credit for.

## The core finding: freshness is a packaging perception, not just a product fact

71% of respondents said a resealable pack made them trust a snack was fresher, even when researchers told them the product inside was identical to what was in a foil pack. This wasn't a subtle effect. It held across every age group tested, and it was strongest among 25 to 34 year olds, at 79%.

That's a striking number for a category where most product and R&D investment goes into what's inside the bag. Shoppers are, in large part, reading freshness off the packaging format itself, not the product.

## The waste this creates is bigger than "spoilage"

54% of respondents said they'd thrown away a partially finished packet of a similar snack in the last month because it "felt stale," and they hadn't even tasted it first to check. The pack alone was enough to trigger disposal.

For brands, this reframes a chunk of what gets bucketed as category waste or shrinkage. It isn't a freshness failure in the product. It's a packaging format that doesn't hold up, visually or tactilely, after first open, and that gap between perception and reality is a design problem with a design solution.

## Income didn't predict the effect, and that changes the pricing conversation

The research team expected wealthier respondents to care more about resealable packaging, on the assumption that it reads as a premium feature. That's not what the data showed. 68% of respondents in the lowest income bracket associated resealable packaging with freshness, compared to 73% in the highest bracket, a gap small enough to treat as noise.

Health consciousness didn't predict it either. Respondents who described themselves as health conscious were no more or less likely to value resealability than anyone else.

Put together, these two null results matter more than they might look at first glance: this isn't a segment-specific preference that only justifies a premium SKU. It's close to universal, which changes how a brand should think about which parts of its portfolio deserve the switch.

## Shoppers will pay for it, and they'll choose it head to head

When shown two identical snacks side by side, one in foil and one in a resealable pouch, 62% picked the resealable pack as the one they'd buy again. 44% said they'd pay more for it outright, with a median acceptable premium of 8%.

That 8% is a real number a brand can run against its actual cost delta for switching from foil to resealable stand-up pouches, not a vague directional signal.

## What this means for brand and packaging teams

The pattern across all five findings points the same direction: packaging format is not a secondary decision that happens after the product is finalized. For a meaningful share of shoppers, it *is* the freshness signal, it drives repeat purchase intent, and it supports a real price premium, all independent of anything happening inside the pack.

Brands weighing a switch from foil to resealable formats now have a data-backed way to prioritize: start with SKUs that see the highest rates of "felt stale" disposal, since that's where the packaging, not the product, is actively working against the brand.
